## Chapter 12. ATOMS — HOW MATTER IS BUILT FROM AOLS AND LUNOLS

We have approached the point where the surrounding world begins to physically assemble into a single whole.
In the previous chapters, two basic elements of aol physics were introduced: ideal spherical aols and lunols — that is, aols that have undergone plastic axial stamping in the centers of Minor universes and received one or two dimples.

Now it is necessary to answer the main question: in what manner are atomic structures formed from this simple set of material elements?
We will design the atom not as a speculative abstraction with virtual electron orbits, probability clouds, and quarks fantastically held together by "glue," but as a real, tangible, and visual mechanical structure assembled from lunols and aols. A structure that will provide a logical rigidity to atoms.

An atomic structure does not possess any special internal properties that force it to behave in a certain way. It is absolutely passive. Its stability, shape, and possible further connections are determined exclusively by the geometry of its constituent elements and the constant external impact of the super-dense aol space.

**Elementary Configurations of Matter: The Structure of Protium and Helium**

The assembly of the first stable material objects begins with the simplest mechanical unit — two aols fixed in the dimple of a lunol by the vibrational pressure of the surrounding environment.

This yields an elementary structure:

**aol + lunol + aol**

However, if single-dimple anomalies arise during the pressing process in the centers of the rhombic dodecahedrons (when an aol is stamped after previously settling into the "socket" of a lunol), the topology of the first elementary matter changes.

The minimum possible and lightest atom in the Universe becomes a pair: one single-dimple lunol and one spherical aol clamped in its sole recess. This configuration represents a mechanical model of the hydrogen atom (protium):

**lunol (single-dimple) + aol**

Structures with one lunol and two aols, as well as a hypothetical "pyramid" structure made of three lunols and three aols, are possible structures for hydrogen isotopes.    
It is crucial that there is no need to introduce a separate substance responsible for a "positive charge," "negative charge," or internal chemical activity. What we have before us is simply a specific geometric assembly of passive material elements.

The next in degree of complexity and ultimate geometric stability is the assembly of the helium atom. In aol physics, this inert element is modeled as a rigid spatial "square," with four lunols arranged along its perimeter and their corner joints securely wedged by four spherical aols.

This configuration represents an absolutely closed, symmetrical, and mechanically monolithic system. Due to the absence of free external dimples, the helium atom is completely devoid of any possibility to enter into further compounds, which perfectly explains its chemical inertness without resorting to the quantum laws of "filled electron shells."

**What Holds Aols in the Dimples**

It is fundamentally important to define the physical mechanism that holds the elements of an atomic structure together.    
In aol physics, this process is entirely cleansed of mystical terms like "internal binding energy," which lacks a mechanical explanation, or "strong interaction" with the incredible, fantastic properties of gluons.

The positioning of elements is determined exclusively by the geometry of the dimple (it is standard for all lunols) and the external, all-encompassing impact of the aol space.
The retention of elements occurs solely due to the external, omnidirectional pressure of the aol space. The hyperactive medium continuously presses upon the structure from all sides, clamping and fixing the elements within the geometrically complementary recesses of the dimples. The mechanism is extremely simple, visual, and clear. No additional, hidden mechanism of retention is required.

However, an atomic structure is not an absolutely immovable and permanently closed system — it is not a "cast-iron slug."
External oscillations of the medium can, under certain conditions, knock aols out of open dimples. Once a dimple is vacated, another aol can instantly slip into it. Therefore, at the micro-world level, a constant mechanical rearrangement of individual elements is possible while preserving the overall stable geometry of the structure. This process resembles a relay-race replacement of balls in a clamped cassette under the continuous blows of a vibratory press.

This means that an atom should be viewed not as an absolutely stationary body, but as a structure that is stable yet dynamically interacting with the surrounding environment.

**The Phenomenon of "Lunol-in-Dimple" Interlocking and Spatial Branching**

The fundamental complication of the architecture of matter and the transition from light elements to heavy atoms begin when the dimple of a lunol receives not an aol, but the spherical surface of another lunol. Since lunols are incredibly abundant in the epicenters of Minor universes, such interlocking of lunols occurs frequently.

Let us analyze the geometry of the resulting joint.    
When one double-dimple lunol settles with its convex spherical part into the dimple of a second one, they form a rigid spatial node:

• The first (receiving) lunol has one of its recesses occupied, while the second remains free.    
• The second (entering) lunol has spent one of its spherical sides on the engagement, but has brought into the system two of its own, absolutely free, diametrically opposite dimples.

The result of the interlocking: the original system, which had only two fixation points, increases the number of free dimples to three upon the joining of the two lunols. A fundamental geometric effect of structural branching emerges.

If only aols dropped into the dimples, the evolution of matter would hit a dead end, stamping out primitive, linear chains (though capable of closing into rings). But the "lunol-in-dimple" interlocking instantly transforms the mechanics of the micro-world into a branching, fractal "tree" of matter.

To the resulting three-dimple node, the medium can simultaneously attach several new structural elements — both aols and subsequent lunols. Each newly embedded lunol creates another branching point of the structure.

New branches begin to radiate from a single center, each capable of multiplying the slots further, forming a vast variety of three-dimensional structures of heavy elements.
The joining of aols and lunols in dimples forms ideal mechanical hinges, providing mobility to parts of the structure, allowing parts of the atom to turn, bend, and rotate. It is this hinge geometry that ensures the elasticity and compliance of soft macro-objects.
It is important to emphasize: lunols have no pre-determined "conscious" or chemical "striving" toward branching.

Branching occurs simply because the geometry of a lunol, after a single interlocking, can leave other dimples accessible to subsequent elements.
Thus, the complexity of atomic structures can grow out of the very geometry of the basic parts assembled by the aol space.

**Boundary Lunols and the Openness of Atomic Structure**

As the structure grows, some of the lunols find themselves inside it, while others are located on the external boundary. It is these external, or boundary, lunols that are of particular importance for the subsequent formation of matter.

Their dimples can be:    
• occupied by aols;    
• temporarily free;    
• accessible for interlocking with other aols or lunols;    
• positioned in such a way that the surrounding environment can impact the aols residing within them.

Consequently, an atomic structure is not determined solely by the quantity of elements entering into it. No less important are their mutual arrangement, orientation, degree of closure, and the accessibility of the boundary dimples.

It is precisely the geometry of the external part of the structure that determines which further reconfigurations are possible at all. This proposition is especially crucial for understanding the subsequent formation of molecules.

External lunols with the aols residing in their dimples can participate in the further reconfiguration of the structure. Upon collision with another atomic structure, the mechanical knocking out of such a boundary aol, its relocation, and the formation of a new position are possible, wherein it becomes a shared element of two atomic structures. This is how a real, purely contact-based mechanical bond between atoms is born — without resorting to mystical "exchanges of electron clouds."

**Mechanical Natural Selection of Spatial Structures**

This mechanics inevitably faces the question: if the variants of angular joints and branchings are theoretically infinite, why do we not observe a chaotic accumulation of giant, ugly, amorphous microstructures in nature? Why is the matter of the Universe rigidly standardized within a strict table of chemical elements? Why is the number of elements just over a hundred?

The reason for the world's stability lies in the action of a rigid mechanical natural selection of spatial forms. This selection is absolutely devoid of mind or purpose. It is a direct result of the laws of dynamic resistance of materials and the structural mechanics of the micro-world.

Every atomic frame assembled in the center of a cell is subjected every split second to high-frequency bombardment by an elastically oscillating and pressing medium. If the geometry of the joint turns out to be unsuccessful, unbalanced, or asymmetrical, the vectors of external collisions create critical stresses and torques. The internal nodes of such a structure cannot withstand the permanent shaking and pressure; the elements slip out of the dimples, and the defective assembly instantly collapses, disassembling back into the aol medium.

If, on the other hand, the spatial geometry of the engagements turns out to be mutually balanced, symmetrical, and successful, the elements begin to mechanically block each other's degrees of freedom. In this case, the pressure of the medium begins to work not on fracturing, but on additional compression and fixation of the frame.

Such successful configurations withstand the universal vibrational hum, stabilize, and persist for billions of years, manifesting themselves in the macro-world as stable chemical elements. Matter is exclusively the geometry and topology of stable connections.

Which atoms are more abundant in nature?    
Short chains are the easiest to form — a lunol and one or two aols, which are modifications of the hydrogen atom (its isotopes).    
The simplest closed system, the helium square, forms fairly easily.    
That is precisely why the overwhelming majority of atoms in the Universe are hydrogen, and there is a fair amount of helium. The entire cosmogony is not the magic of the Big Bang, but simple survival statistics of geometric nodes under the hydraulic press of the medium.

**The Limit of Mechanical Stability and Radioactive Decay**

The law of mechanical form selection automatically explains the upper limit of stability for the table of elements. As the atomic "tree" complicates and its fractal branches grow, the structure becomes excessively cumbersome. In super-heavy structures, the number of boundary, weakly fixed nodes increases, and the probability of a geometric misalignment rises. The colossal cumulative load from external collisions of the medium begins to shake the peripheral branches of the giant structure.

When destabilization reaches a critical threshold, the heavy structure naturally shatters (splits) into several more compact, geometrically closed, and stable blocks. In the macro-world, this purely mechanical disassembly of an unstable atomic structure under the blows of the medium is interpreted by official science as the phenomenon of radioactive decay. A critically overloaded lever of the structure breaks, and some parts fly out of their slots under the action of the vibratory pressure of the medium.

This does not require the introduction of a special mystical mechanism fundamentally different from the mechanics of the structure's formation itself. Both the assembly and the breakdown of matter obey a general rule: the medium compresses only that which fits well, and inevitably destroys everything that is cumbersome and unbalanced.

**The Atom as an Open Dynamic Structure**

The atom in the aol model is not an absolutely closed and independent object. It cannot exist by itself in the absolute void of a vacuum. It exists strictly within the aol space, continuously interacts with it, and finds itself under its permanent impact.

Therefore, the atom:        
• is formed under specific environmental conditions;    
• is held together by external omnidirectional pressure;    
• experiences continuous, high-frequency vibrations;    
• can exchange individual structural elements with the surrounding medium;    
• is capable of reconfiguring;    
• can disintegrate under certain conditions.

This does not mean that the atom loses its identity at every single moment. A stable atomic configuration is determined not by the immutability of each individual aol, but by the preservation of the structure's overall geometric organization.

Just as a mechanical system can retain its form during the dynamic replacement of individual parts, an atomic structure can preserve its general type of geometry during a continuous relay-race rearrangement of individual elements. New environment aols fly into the "cassette" of the atom's frame, knocking out the old ones, but the geometric coordinates of the slots and the overall dimensions of the LEGO-structure themselves remain unchanged for billions of years, ensuring the stability of the atom.

Understanding the atom as a mechanical structure shifts its configuration from the realm of abstract entities into the domain of geometry, mechanics, and interaction with the medium.

The core propositions are as follows:

Every physical process becomes a visual and calculable process of geometric reconfiguration, deformation, or disassembly and reassembly of mechanical structures.





## Chapter 13. MOLECULES — HOW ATOMS ASSEMBLE INTO MATTER

An atom is not an abstract "ball," but a concrete mechanical structure assembled from aols and lunols. However, atoms do not exist in isolation. They reside within the aol space and can collide, move relative to one another, and interact through their external, boundary parts.

A subsequent fundamental question arises: how do two independent atomic structures transform into a single molecular structure?

In the aol model, there is no need to introduce a separate mystical mechanism for a chemical bond. A molecule emerges as a result of an ordinary mechanical reconfiguration of already existing joints between aols and lunols.

**1. Atoms Are Not in a Vacuum**

Atoms are immersed in the aol space. They are constantly impacted by the surrounding environment:    
• an enormous external pressure is exerted;  
• ultra-high-frequency vibrations are transmitted;  
• numerous collisions with elements of the medium occur;  
• the atomic structures themselves are in continuous motion.

Therefore, atoms are not stationary objects that simply exist next to each other. They constantly change their position and permanently collide.

During such a collision, it is primarily the external parts of the atomic structures that interact — the boundary lunols and the aols residing in their dimples. It is precisely here that the possibility of a molecule's formation arises.

**2. What Happens When Atoms Collide**

Let us consider two atoms moving toward each other. Their external structures come into contact. Various outcomes are possible.
In the simplest scenario, the collision does not lead to a reconfiguration. The atomic structures transfer motion to one another, change their trajectories, and diverge. However, if the geometry of the boundary lunols' arrangement turns out to be suitable, the collision can result in knocking an aol out of one of the boundary dimples. This is a pivotal moment.

An aol that resided in the dimple of one atom is not a permanently fixed component of that structure. As demonstrated in the previous chapter, an external impact is capable of displacing an aol from its dimple. Upon its exit, the vacated dimple gains the opportunity to participate in a new configuration.

If another atom with a suitable boundary lunol and its own aol is nearby, the possibility of an instant reconfiguration of joints emerges.

**3. A Shared Aol — The Primary Mechanism of Atomic Bonding**

Let us assume that two atoms possess boundary lunols between which a matching geometry arises during collision.
The aol originally located in the dimple of one of the first atom's lunols can be knocked out and displaced to the side, while the aol in the dimple of the second atom's lunol will enter the vacated dimple of the first atom's lunol and become a shared aol of the two atoms.

A joint arises:    
**lunol — aol — lunol**    
where the left and right lunols already belong to different atoms, and the central aol becomes a shared element of their structure.

Thus, two originally independent atoms acquire a shared material element. This is the primary proposed mechanism for molecule formation.

No new physical entity arises here. No special "chemical bond force" appears. There is no need to introduce a separate type of material connection. Only a rearrangement of already existing elements occurs: an aol that belonged to one joint becomes, after mechanical reconfiguration, a shared element of two structures. The structure is tightened by the external pressure of the medium through a single shared ball-lock.

**4. Why This Can Be Viewed as a Continuation of Atomic Assembly**

Within an atomic structure, aols are already joining with lunols. During molecule formation, the exact same thing occurs. The only difference is that now two lunols belong to different atomic structures.

Therefore, the building chain of matter maintains a single principle:    
**aol → lunol → atom → shared aol between atoms → molecule**

A molecule does not require a new physical mechanism compared to an atom. It represents the next level of the same mechanical assembly.

This is particularly important for the aol model: a chemical compound is not introduced as an additional fundamental entity, but is derived from the already existing geometry of aols and lunols.

**5. The Role of Boundary Lunols**

Not every lunol within an atomic structure is capable of participating in the formation of an interatomic joint. A large portion of the lunols in a complex atom can reside inside the structure and remain inaccessible for direct interaction with another atom. For molecule formation, it is primarily the boundary lunols that matter.

Their positioning determines:    
• from which side the atom can come into contact with another structure;  
• which dimples are accessible;  
• which aols can be knocked out;  
• in which direction the reconfiguration can occur;  
• what position the shared aol will occupy after joining.

Thus, the possibility of a molecule's formation is determined not by some "chemical affinity" or an internal "striving" of the atom to bond, but exclusively by the geometry of its external structure. The atom remains an absolutely passive material system operating under the laws of spatial tightness.

**6. Different Numbers of Shared Aols**

Branched atomic structures can possess several boundary lunols. Therefore, depending on their geometry, more than one shared aol can arise between two atoms. In this case, the joining of the two atomic structures is accomplished no longer through a single material element, but through several shared material elements.

For example, schematically:    
**lunol — aol — lunol**    
can be just one of the connecting nodes between two atoms.

In the presence of several suitable boundary lunols, multiple such nodes can arise:    
**lunol — aol — lunol**  
**lunol — aol — lunol**  
and so on.

The more independent geometric nodes connect the two structures, the more their mutual motion is restricted. Therefore, the strength and stability of a molecular structure can depend on the quantity, arrangement, and geometry of the shared aols.

This does not mean that the atom possesses a pre-determined number of "bonds." The number of possible connections is a direct consequence of the specific geometry of the atomic structure.

**7. Possible Direct Interlocking of Lunols**

Another geometric variant is also possible, which requires separate consideration.
If the aol closing the dimple of a boundary lunol has been knocked out, the vacated dimple could theoretically turn out to be capable of receiving not a free aol, but the spherical part of a lunol belonging to another atom.

Schematically:    
**lunol dimple ← spherical part of another lunol**

In such a case, the connection occurs directly between the two lunols. However, this variant should not yet be considered an established mechanism of molecular bond formation. For this option, it is necessary to separately verify the geometry, the possibility of stable fixation, and the behavior of such a structure under the action of external pressure and vibrations of the aol space.

Therefore, the primary mechanism of molecular compound formation in this model is viewed through a shared aol, while the direct interlocking of lunols remains a possible additional variant requiring further research.

**8. What Holds Atoms Together in a Molecule**

After the formation of a shared connecting node, the external pressure of the aol space continues to act upon the entire structure. It presses the constituent elements against one another and prevents the spontaneous exit of the shared aol from its corresponding dimples.

Therefore, a molecular structure is held together by the exact same physical principle that retains the individual elements of an atom:    
**geometry of the joint + external pressure of the aol space**

A molecule does not require a separate long-range, non-contact force that specifically arises between atoms. If the geometric configuration is stable, the pressure of the medium maintains it. If the configuration is unstable, it rearranges or disintegrates.

**9. Why Not Just Any Atoms Combine**

If the formation of molecules is a mechanical reconfiguration, a legitimate question arises: why do two arbitrary atoms not combine during every collision? The answer lies within geometry.

For the formation of a stable shared joint, the simultaneous fulfillment of several conditions is required:    
• a suitable mutual position of the atoms;  
• accessibility of the boundary lunols;  
• the possibility of knocking out the corresponding aol;  
• the possibility of its transition to a new position;  
• geometric compatibility of the new joints;  
• mechanical stability of the resulting structure.

If these conditions are not met, the collision ends with an ordinary change in the motion of the atoms. Therefore, chemical diversity can spring from an immense number of distinct geometric configurations.

**10. The Role of Medium Vibrations**

The aol space is not a stationary medium. Its vibrations constantly act upon atomic and molecular structures. Following the formation of a molecule, these vibrations are transmitted throughout the entire combined structure.

If the vibrational impact is sufficiently large, the shared aol can be knocked out of one of the dimples. The interatomic joint then disintegrates. In the macro-world, this purely mechanical ejection of the stopper under the influence of the increased shaking of the medium is termed by official chemistry as the thermal dissociation of a molecule.

However, another outcome is also possible: during the motion of the structure, one or several elements can occupy a new stable position, after which a different molecular configuration is formed. Therefore, molecules should be viewed as dynamic mechanical structures rather than absolutely immovable formations.

**11. Strength of the Molecular Structure**

The strength of a molecule is determined by its geometry. If two atoms are connected by a single shared aol, their mutual position is constrained by a single mechanical node.

If several shared aols exist between them, multiple simultaneously operating nodes arise. Their spatial arrangement is also significant. Two joints identical in the number of connecting nodes can yield completely different stability if they are arranged differently.

Therefore, the strength of a molecule is a consequence of:    
• the quantity of shared aols;  
• the position of these aols;  
• the geometry of the lunols' arrangement;  
• the mutual orientation of the atomic structures;  
• the pressure and high-frequency vibrations of the surrounding environment.

Thus, strength is again determined not by an abstract property of the atoms, but by the specific geometry of the assembled structure.

**12. Chemical Reaction as a Reconfiguration of Molecular Structures**

Now a chemical reaction can be defined within the framework of the aol model.
A chemical reaction is the mechanical reconfiguration of existing material structures.

When molecules or atoms collide, the following can occur:    
• knocking out of aols from their former dimples;  
• formation of new shared aols;  
• destruction of old interatomic joints;  
• formation of new joints between other atoms;  
• alteration of the spatial configuration of the entire system.

Therefore, a chemical reaction does not require the introduction of a separate mechanism. It is a sequence of ordinary mechanical reconfigurations.

It can be conceptualized as follows:    
**initial atomic joints → collision → reconfiguration of aol–lunol joints → new geometry → new molecule**

It is precisely the alteration of the geometry of material elements that constitutes the physical substance of a chemical reaction in this model.

**13. Why Stable Molecules Are Formed**

Following a collision, numerous intermediate configurations are possible. Most of them may turn out to be unstable. If the shared aol easily exits the dimple, the joint disintegrates. If the new configuration poorly withstands the vibrations of the medium, it is likewise destroyed.

However, certain configurations prove to be mechanically stable. They persist longer than others and can form anew when the corresponding conditions recur.

Thus arises the observed diversity of stable molecular structures. In this picture, chemical stability is a direct consequence of the structural geometry's stability.

**14. Destruction of Molecules**

A molecule can exist only as long as its mechanically stable configuration is preserved. If the impact of the medium becomes sufficiently strong, one or several shared aols can leave their dimples. The bonding of the atoms is disrupted.

From there, two primary outcomes are possible:    
1.	the molecule breaks down into the initial atomic structures;  
2.	the vacated elements enter into new joints and form a different molecular structure.

Consequently, the destruction of a molecule and a chemical reaction are processes of the same class. In both cases, an exclusively spatial reconfiguration of material joints occurs.

**15. The Molecule Is the Next Stage of Matter Assembly**

An atom is a mechanical structure made of aols and lunols. A molecule is a mechanical structure in which several atomic structures are connected to each other by means of the exact same material elements.

The principle remains unified:    
• aols are fixed within the dimples of lunols;  
• during collisions, a reconfiguration of these joints is possible;  
• boundary aols can become shared by the lunols of different atoms;  
• the stability of the resulting structure is determined by its geometry and the impact of the surrounding space.

Thus, the transition    
**aol → lunol → atom → molecule**  
does not require the introduction of a new fundamental mechanism at each level.   
The complex emerges from the simple by means of a sequential reconfiguration of one and the same material geometry.

**CHAPTER SUMMARY**

In the aol model, a molecule is not the result of a special long-range, non-contact "chemical force," but rather the next stage in the mechanical assembly of matter.

The core propositions are as follows:    
• Atoms are absolutely passive material structures.  
• Their behavior is determined strictly by their geometry and the impact of the aol space.  
• During collisions, it is primarily the boundary lunols and the aols residing within them that interact.  
• An aol can be knocked out of its former dimple and occupy a new position.  
• The primary mechanism of interatomic bonding is the formation of a shared aol residing in the dimples of two different atomic structures (the **"lunol — aol — lunol"** chain).  
• Branched atoms can possess several boundary lunols and are therefore capable of forming multiple shared connecting nodes, thus establishing multiple bonds.  
• The quantity and arrangement of such nodes are determined exclusively by the geometry of the structure, rather than a pre-determined "property" of the atom.  
• The possibility of the direct entry of a spherical part of one lunol into a vacated dimple of another lunol is permitted as a possible additional mechanism, but requires separate engineering research.  
• The stability of a molecule is determined by the geometry of its joints and the external pressure of the aol space.  
• A chemical reaction constitutes a purely mechanical reconfiguration of aol–lunol joints and an alteration of the overall geometry of the material structure.

Thus, the molecule is a continuation of the exact same sequence from which the atom emerges:    
**aol → lunol → atom → shared aol between atoms → molecule**

Matter does not receive a new principle at each level of complexity.
It assembles.








## Chapter 14. GAS, LIQUID, AND SOLID — HOW MATTER BEHAVES

Around us lies the familiar macroscopic world. We are enveloped by air — light, transparent, and intangible. We interact with water — it flows, fills vessels, and readily assumes the shape of any container. We lean upon a stone — solid, heavy, monolithic, and preserving its geometry under external loads.

Since childhood, school textbooks have trained us to classify this reality through the dogma of three isolated "aggregate states of matter": gas, liquid, and solid.  
Yet, what constitutes their true, deep physical difference? If absolutely all matter in the Universe is constructed from identical primary atoms assembled from aols and lunols, then for what reason do the exact same structural units behave so qualitatively differently in space?

This is where the fundamental mechanistic understanding of the nature of substance takes its root. Official science draws rigid, speculative boundaries between phases, bound together by complex thermodynamic equations. But if one thinks in categories of local action (contact mechanics), a fatal logical flaw is exposed within these boundaries.  
At what specific microscopic instant does water cease to be a liquid and turn into vapor? Within what fraction of a second does heated metal lose the properties of a solid body and begin to flow?

In reality, no mystical, step-like phase barriers exist. There is only a smooth, continuous alteration in the dynamic behavior of the exact same material structures.

This behavior is entirely dictated by the balance of two mechanical factors:

•	The degrees of strength and mutual locking of the aol-lunol engagements within the structure.  
•	The intensity of the destructive, deconstructive bombardment from the hyperactive, vibrating, and pressing surrounding medium.

All the properties of matter familiar to us are derived from the preservation or violation of this force equilibrium.

**1. The Gaseous State: The Regime of Free Trajectories**

Let us visualize the real physical picture of the process. The aol space is completely filled with a super-dense array of elastic, rigid spheres. They exist in a state of permanent, high-frequency oscillation, transmit colossal cumulative pressure, and generate the through-and-through vibrational background of the Universe. Atomic and molecular structures are immersed inside this hyperactive medium. 

They are not static background decorations — every single second, they take on trillions of impacts (contemplate this number) from the elements of the medium, colliding, dispersing, locking together, and reconfiguring. This is a continuous, non-decaying mechanical process.

If the strength of the engagement between individual atomic structures is weak, while the external oscillatory bombardment from the medium is extremely intense, a stable spatial bond between the atoms has no opportunity to become locked. Colliding with one another and with the aols of space, the molecules elastically transfer kinetic momentum and instantaneously scatter in all directions along chaotic trajectories.

This is how gas manifests itself. A gaseous medium is incapable of retaining its shape, tends to occupy the entire available volume of space, and easily yields to compression. This occurs not due to mystical quantum laws, but for a purely mechanical reason: its structural elements are linked together extremely weakly or are not fixed at all, and the distances between them are substantial — meaning they can easily be brought closer to one another by elevated pressure.

**2. The Liquid State: The Regime of Dynamic Play**

When the external action of the medium becomes more moderate, and the elastic pressing of the aol space begins to effectively sustain the mating nodes of the conjoined particles, the physical picture changes. Atomic structures acquire the capacity to link with one another; however, this fixation is not yet rigid and deadlocked.

Under the impacts of the medium’s high-frequency oscillations, individual intermolecular mechanical locks disconnect, but they immediately snap shut into new spatial positions due to the external press of the medium. The structure exists as a volumetric whole, yet retains internal mobility.

This is how liquid manifests itself. It is no longer capable of dispersing in space like a gas, since the external pressure of the medium sustains its overall volume, but it does not yet possess the rigid framework of a solid body. A liquid maintains a stable physical volume, yet instantaneously alters its geometry and flows under the action of the slightest force gradients, obeying the laws of dynamic play of continuously reassembling components.

**3. The Solid Body: The Regime of Spatial Locking of Degrees of Freedom**

If the intensity of the medium's oscillatory bombardment falls below a critical threshold, while the external omnidirectional pressing of aols deadlocks the atomic components into the complementary recesses of neighboring atoms, a fundamentally different topology crystallizes within the Universe.  
A stable, stationary spatial network of mutual atomic retentions arises. Every individual component of the structure turns out to be rigidly clamped, supported, and locked from all sides by neighboring elements.

Thus a solid body is born. The resulting monolithic framework preserves its specified shape, effectively resists any shear and deformational macro-loads, and the vibrational pressure of space is no longer capable of fracturing the engagement nodes.  
The structure translates the external impacts of the medium into a regime of safe, localized internal micro-oscillations of its elements around their fixation points.

Phase transitions of substance are not the magic of transforming one mysterious entity into another.
