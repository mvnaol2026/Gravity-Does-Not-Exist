## Chapter 7. AOLIC SPACE AND THE STRUCTURE OF SMALL UNIVERSES

After introducing aols as the primary elements of the world, the next step is to look not at a single aol, but at the medium as a whole.
Because aols by themselves do not yet explain the structure of the universe.
They only provide us with the “substance of space.” 

Now we must understand how this substance is organized—why it is under pressure, why it vibrates continuously, why in some regions of the world conditions arise for the formation of matter, while in others they do not.

At this point, we arrive at one of the most important ideas in the entire aolic model: the concept of the universe as a system of Small Universes, each representing an immense region of aolic space with its own internal mechanics.

**1. Why is it not enough to assume a “uniform infinite medium”**

At first glance, one might say: space is filled with aols, the medium exists, it is dense, it exerts pressure—and that should be sufficient.
But if we stop there, an immediate question arises: where does the pressure come from?

If the medium is infinite and has no structure anywhere, it becomes difficult to explain why any directed distribution of pressure should arise at all. Why does everything not simply balance out immediately? Why do regions with different conditions exist? Where does the tendency for pressure to increase in some areas and decrease in others come from?

To explain this, the idea of “just a medium” is not enough.
A more complex organization is required.
This is why the aolic model introduces the idea that the infinite universe is not a chaotic expanse, but is instead composed of immense regions — **Small Universes.**

**2. What is a Small Universe**
When we speak of the universe, it is often imagined as an infinite space in which galaxies, stars, and other objects are distributed chaotically.

But chaos is only an appearance.
Matter does not tend toward chaos. It tends toward equilibrium. And equilibrium is achieved not through disorder, but through organization.

This is a fundamental property of matter: self-organization.
Matter in motion, under the influence of interactions, begins to reorganize itself in such a way as to reduce the overall intensity of processes, to decrease the intensity of motion, and to move toward a more stable state.

In physics, this is often described as a tendency toward the minimization of energy. In our model, it is more precise to speak of the minimization of motion and pressure.

Such processes of self-organization are well known.
One need only recall Bénard cells.
When a liquid is heated from below, chaotic motion first appears. But then the system reorganizes—and stable structures emerge in the form of hexagonal cells. The motion does not disappear, but it becomes organized and ordered.

The system finds a configuration in which it is “easier to exist.”
The same principle operates in aolic space.

Aolic space vibrates continuously. Impacts are constantly transmitted, interactions are constantly occurring. In such a state, complete equilibrium is unattainable, but the system tends toward the most stable possible configuration.

And it finds it.
The universe becomes partitioned into immense cells—Small Universes.
These cells take the form of rhombic dodecahedra.

This is not a random shape.
The rhombic dodecahedron is one of the few geometries capable of filling space without gaps. Each cell fits tightly against its neighbors, leaving no voids. The entire universe becomes completely filled with such structures.

This is the three-dimensional analogue of Bénard cells.
Except that instead of flat hexagons, we now have volumetric rhombic dodecahedra.
Why does this particular structure turn out to be stable?
Because it allows the system to distribute vibrational pressure in the most uniform way.

At the boundaries of the Small Universes, the pressure is minimal.
There, neighboring structures balance one another.

But as one moves toward the center of each cell, the situation changes.
Each inner layer of aols receives pressure from outer layers that contain a greater number of aols. As a result, pressure gradually increases toward the center.

The center of a Small Universe becomes a region of maximum pressure and maximum vibrational intensity.
Thus, a stable configuration emerges: minimal pressure at the periphery, maximal pressure at the center.
And this structure sustains itself.

Aolic space does not “build” it deliberately. It simply, being in a state of constant vibration, evolves toward a distribution that proves to be the most stable.
This is precisely what is meant by synergetics—the self-organization of matter.

The large-scale universe turns out not to be chaotic, but structured. It consists of an infinite number of Small Universes, each with its own internal dynamics, yet connected to its neighbors through shared boundaries.

And it is precisely within these Small Universes—at their centers, where pressure is maximal—that the processes of formation begin: the emergence of lunols, atoms, matter, and all the structures we observe.
Then, as one moves toward the periphery, these structures break down, returning once again to the state of aols.

Thus, the universe is not a collection of random objects, but a unified self-organizing system in which form, motion, and pressure are interconnected.
And this system continuously sustains itself.

By a “Small Universe,” we do not mean a “small world” in any everyday sense. The name may be misleading. What is meant is an immense region of space, whose size may extend over billions of light-years.

Why, then, is it called “small”?
Because it is not the entire infinite universe, but only one of its cells—one of its vast structural units. Viewed against the totality of an infinite universe, such a region is a local unit, a single enormous volume within an even larger structure.

**3. Why a cellular structure?**

If Small Universes exist, the immediate question is: what shape must they have?
This cannot be chosen arbitrarily. The shape must satisfy several conditions at once.

First, Small Universes must fill all of space without gaps. Otherwise, emptiness would reappear between them, contradicting the entire logic of the model.

Second, each Small Universe must be able to contact its neighbors in such a way that a minimal, initial pressure arises at the boundaries.

Third, within each of them, there must be a natural possibility for pressure to increase from the periphery toward the center.

Not every geometric form satisfies these requirements.
Spheres, for example, are appealing in imagination, but they do not fill space without voids. Cubes do fill space, but they do not align well with the idea of a natural central intensification of pressure in a system where near-sphericity and uniform transmission of interaction are important.

In the aolic model, the most natural form is the rhombic dodecahedron.

**4. Where does the initial peripheral pressure come from**

If we reason in simple terms, the picture is as follows.
We have an infinite system of Small Universes, formed through the self-organization of vibrating matter seeking equilibrium. Each of them is in contact with its neighbors. At their boundaries, there is no emptiness. There is contact between faces. And there is the vibration of aolic space, driven by the ongoing search for equilibrium. Therefore, there is pressure—vibrational pressure.

Small Universes, supporting one another, create a certain pressure at their contact boundaries. This pressure settles at a minimal level as equilibrium is approached, while maximal pressure accumulates at the centers of the rhombic dodecahedra.

It is this peripheral pressure, established between Small Universes, that becomes the starting point for all subsequent vibrational mechanics.

**5. Why does pressure increase toward the center**

Let us imagine how pressure from the periphery is transmitted inward within a Small Universe. In the outer layers of aols, pressure is distributed across a very large number of elements. But as we move closer to the center, the number of aols in each successive inner layer becomes smaller, while the same transmitted pressure continues to pass through them and accumulate.

At this point, an important effect appears.
In simple terms, it is as if “many press upon a few.”
Of course, this is an image rather than an exact geometric description. But it captures the essence. The outer layer contains more elements and transmits pressure to an inner layer composed of fewer elements. As a result, each aol in the inner layer receives a larger share of the total pressure.

This process continues inward.
Each successive layer, closer to the center, bears an increasingly greater load from the more numerous outer layers.
Thus, pressure naturally increases toward the center.

**6. Vibrations of aolic space**

Aolic space does not merely exert pressure — it also vibrates.
These vibrations are not something separate from pressure. They arise naturally from it. Wherever there is a complex medium under stress, there are oscillations. Wherever there is a tendency toward equilibrium combined with the impossibility of fully achieving it, vibration inevitably appears.

For this reason, aolic space should not be imagined as a static, solid mass, but as a dynamic, continuously oscillating medium.

This oscillation is universal. It is present everywhere. It forms the background of the world.
Later, when we speak about light, heat, electricity, and many other phenomena, it will become clear that these vibrations lie at the foundation of numerous processes.

Before moving on to lunols (deformed aols with two diametrically opposite indentations), it is useful to anticipate the question: why does aolic space never “settle,” never reach a perfectly stable state, but instead continues to vibrate?

The answer lies in the fact that there are always factors present that disturb equilibrium.
The first such factor is the existence of lunols. Their very formation already represents a disturbance. An aol is a sphere, ideally integrated into the structure of the medium. A lunol, however, is a distorted aol. Its indentations are geometric defects that inevitably disrupt the uniformity of pressure and the arrangement of surrounding aols. The very process of forming lunols makes the medium non-ideal.

The second factor is all structures built from aols and lunols: atoms, molecules, bodies, planets, stars. Any structure is already a disturbance of uniformity. It alters the distribution of pressure, creates local imbalances, and redistributes flows of interaction. The larger the object, the more strongly it disrupts the uniformity of the medium.

There is also a third factor, which manifests most clearly in the peripheral regions of the Small Universes.
This is the re-shaping of lunols.

When objects — stars and planets—break down at the periphery, lunols are released. They are no longer bound within atoms, no longer protected by structures. They are exposed directly to aolic space.
And here, an intense process begins.

A lunol is a deformed aol. Its indentations inevitably capture aols from the surrounding medium. But once inside, these aols do not remain there permanently, because the pressure is insufficient to hold them in place. They are knocked out by subsequent impacts, collide with the edges of the indentations, and pass impulses further along. Each such entry and ejection is a microscopic impact — a local disturbance.

And such events occur in enormous numbers.
The flow of impacts in aolic space never ceases, not even for a moment. The frequencies involved are immense—on the order of 10¹³ hertz. This means that every lunol is continuously “bombarded” from all directions. Each indentation becomes a source of chaotic micro-interactions: aols enter, are ejected, strike the edges, and generate local deformations.

The re-shaping of a lunol is not a smooth process of gradual correction. It is a violent, impact-driven process.
And when many lunols are present, the peripheral regions become zones where the medium is effectively “boiling” with microscopic disturbances.

Thus, the periphery is not merely a region of reduced pressure. It is also a region of active generation of vibrations. Local disturbances are constantly created here and transmitted throughout aolic space.
A closed cycle emerges.

At the centers of Small Universes, under immense pressure, lunols are formed. From them arise atoms and more complex structures. These structures drift toward the periphery. There, the pressure is no longer sufficient to sustain them, and they break apart. Lunols are released and begin to re-shape. In the process, they generate new vibrations. And eventually, having restored their form, they return to being aols — the original state of the medium.

Matter does not accumulate defects indefinitely. It continually returns to its original form.
From this follows another important conclusion.

**Atoms, molecules, bodies, planets — all of these are not eternal formations.** They exist for long periods — billions of years — but not forever. Each atom has its own path: from its formation in regions of maximal pressure to its decay in peripheral regions.
They merely make temporary use of the aols from which they are composed.

One might say that atoms “borrow” aols from aolic space.
But the aols themselves are eternal.
It is precisely this permanence of aols, together with the continuous formation and re-shaping of lunols, that makes aolic space a living, constantly vibrating medium — one that never comes to rest.

Equilibrium within this system is not impossible in principle — it is impossible in practice because it is continuously disrupted by the very nature of the processes taking place.
And in this lies the source of all the oscillations we observe in the universe.

Let us try to grasp the scale of these vibrations.
Human perception is inherently “slow.” We see motion, hear sound, feel heat. But all of this belongs to a very low-frequency layer of processes.

If we turn instead to other phenomena—those already known to science—we notice something remarkable.
Atoms are known to vibrate at extremely high frequencies.

We are dealing with values on the order of 10¹³ oscillations per second and above.
These are not occasional fluctuations.
They are the constant state of matter. Even so-called “zero-point oscillations” at absolute zero occur at frequencies on the order of 10¹³ hertz.

If atoms exist in such a state of vibration, a natural question arises: where does it come from?
Within the aolic model, the answer is straightforward.
**Atoms do not “vibrate by themselves.”**

They exist within a vibrating medium.
It is aolic space that transmits these oscillations to them.
In other words, **atoms are not the source of vibration — their motion is a consequence of the vibration of aolic space.**

**7. Central regions of Small Universes**

If pressure truly increases toward the center, then the central regions of Small Universes must be special zones.
And this is indeed the case.

In these regions, pressure reaches enormous magnitudes. Not at a mathematical point, but across vast volumes—regions of truly immense scale, far beyond any human frame of reference.
Under these conditions, some aols begin to deform.
Not all. Not everywhere. Not arbitrarily.
Only where the pressure becomes sufficiently great.

It is here that the conditions arise for the formation of indentations, and for the transformation of ordinary aols into lunols.
Thus, the central regions of Small Universes become the sites where stable matter begins to form.

**8. Why does matter originate here**

As long as the pressure is insufficient, aols remain in their ordinary state—smooth, spherical, capable of transmitting pressure, but not yet capable of forming stable mechanical interlocking.

But when pressure reaches critical levels, some aols begin to undergo irreversible deformation. Indentations appear. Lunols are formed. And from this point onward, the formation of atoms, molecules, and all subsequent structures becomes possible.

This means that matter, in the form familiar to us, does not exist eternally in a ready-made state. It arises in specific regions of aolic space—where the necessary conditions are present.
And those conditions are created by the medium itself, through its pressure.

**9. Motion from the center to the periphery**

When matter forms, it does not remain forever in the central region.
The medium continues to act upon it—and this action has direction.
If pressure increases toward the center, then for already formed structures there exists a tendency to be pushed outward—from the center toward the periphery. Matter is expelled from the region of maximum compression.

This will later play a key role in explaining large-scale cosmic motion.
But for now, it is important to establish the following:
A Small Universe is not a static container filled uniformly.

It is a dynamic system within which the following occurs:

— an increase of pressure toward the center,

— the formation of matter in central regions,

— the subsequent outward motion of that matter.


**10. What happens at the periphery**

At the periphery, the situation is reversed.
There, the pressure is no longer sufficient to maintain complex structures in a stable form. If matter in the center is assembled, compressed, and formed, then closer to the periphery conditions arise for its weakening, breakdown, and decay.

Thus, a Small Universe as a whole behaves like a vast mechanical cycle: formation at the center, existence and motion in intermediate regions, and weakening and decay at the periphery.
This idea will become very important later, when we turn to the cosmology of the aolic model.

**11. Why this picture matters**

At first glance, all of this may seem overly large in scope—almost cosmological speculation. But in reality, without this framework, the following chapters cannot be properly understood.

Because here, several essential elements are established at once:

1. Space acquires a real structure.
2. Pressure ceases to be an abstraction and gains a source.
3. Vibrations become a natural property of the medium.
4. A mechanism for the formation of matter emerges.
5. A unified cosmic context appears for all subsequent processes.

Without this, light, heat, electricity, and even gravitation would appear as disconnected phenomena.
With this framework, they begin to assemble into a single coherent world.

**12. The main conclusion**

Now we can state the central point.
The universe is not emptiness in which objects are randomly scattered.
Nor is it merely an infinite, uniform medium.

It is an infinite system of Small Universes, shaped as rhombic dodecahedra, in contact with one another and thereby generating an initial minimal pressure at their boundaries.

This pressure, transmitted inward and accumulated across progressively fewer aols in the inner layers, increases toward the centers of the Small Universes.

As a result, aolic space is:

— dense,

— continuous,

— vibrating,

— and subject to non-uniform pressure.

And it is precisely within such a medium that the emergence of matter, the motion of bodies, and all the processes we call physical phenomena become possible.




## Chapter 8. WHY CELLS OF SMALL UNIVERSES ARISE FROM A CHAOTIC DENSE MEDIUM

It would be unnatural to assume that the primary medium of nature exists in a perfectly ordered packing, where every aol has the maximum number of contacts and the entire structure is infinitely symmetrical.

Such states are convenient for geometry, but they are rarely found in real nature.
Natural systems usually form not as ideal blueprints, but as living structures containing local deviations, distortions, and statistical distributions of elements.

Therefore, it is more natural to suppose that aolian space is not an ideal crystal, but a dense yet not fully ordered packing of aols.

One aol may have 4 contacts, another 7, a third 10, and some up to 12.
This means pressure is distributed unevenly.
Some aols are compressed more strongly, others more weakly. Somewhere there is microscopic play. Somewhere contact is nearly direct, elsewhere it occurs at an angle.
It is precisely such a medium that can possess internal dynamics, rearrangements, and vibrations.

In such a medium:

— the number of contacts differs from one aol to another,

— pressure is distributed unevenly,

— micro-displacements arise,

— vibrations and rearrangements occur.

That means the medium cannot remain in complete rest.

But then the next question arises.
If a system constantly experiences internal stresses, what will it tend toward?
Nature’s answer is almost always the same: it will tend toward a more stable organization.

**1. Chaos Is Rarely the Final State**

If many elements interact for long periods, press against one another, and are able to rearrange, a disordered state usually does not persist forever.

The system begins to:

— redistribute stresses,

— equalize loads,

— seek configurations with fewer internal conflicts.

This can be seen in many processes:

— bubble structures
,
— cracks in drying surfaces,

— Bénard cells,

— granular media.

Nature prefers not chaos, but stable order.

**2. What Is Required of the Structure of Aolian Space**

If the medium is infinite and completely filled, then any large-scale structure that emerges must satisfy several conditions.

It must:

— fill space without voids,

— contact neighboring regions uniformly,

— transmit pressure efficiently,

— avoid unnecessary zones of overstress,

— remain stable under vibrations.

Not every shape is suitable for this.

**3. Why Not a Sphere**

A sphere is beautiful and natural as an individual object.
But spheres cannot fill space without voids.
Gaps would remain between them.
And for a model in which emptiness is excluded, this is a problem.

**4. Why Not Random Shapes**

Random polyhedra may arise temporarily, but they distribute pressure poorly.

They have:

— unequal faces,

— different angles,

— weak structural repeatability.

Such a system is less stable.

**5. Why Specifically the Rhombic Dodecahedron**

The Rhombic dodecahedron possesses a rare combination of properties.

It:

— fills space completely without gaps,

— is close in spirit to a sphere (with sufficiently rounded symmetry),

— has many neighboring contacts,

— distributes loads efficiently across its faces,

— allows regular structural repetition.

In other words, it is a compromise between packing density and uniform pressure transmission.

**6. How Such a Form Could Arise Naturally**

It is important to understand that there is no need to assume the medium “knew geometry.”
Something else happens.
Initially, there are many chaotic pressure regions.

Those configurations that distribute loads poorly:

— break down,

— rearrange,

— give way to more stable ones.

And configurations that better damp internal distortions:

— persist,

— expand,

— repeat themselves.

This is a natural selection of forms.

**7. Why Cells Appear**

If a stable volume comes into contact with neighboring volumes of the same kind, the medium begins to divide into regions.

Each region:

— is internally coordinated,

— has contact boundaries with its neighbors,

— participates in the common transmission of pressure.

Thus a cellular universe emerges.
Not as an artificial grid, but as the result of self-stabilization of the medium.

**8. Why Pressure Increases Toward the Center of a Cell**

After a stable cell forms, its periphery contacts neighboring cells.
There, pressure is mutually balanced.
But deeper inside, pressure from the outer layers is transmitted to an ever smaller number of inner aols.

As a result, the load naturally concentrates toward the center.
Thus the center becomes the zone of maximum pressure.

**9. Main Conclusion**

The Rhombic dodecahedron in the aolian model is not decorative geometry.

It is one of the most natural forms that can arise in a dense vibrating medium seeking to:

— fill space without voids,

— equalize pressure,

— preserve stability.

That is why, from a chaotic dense medium, one should reasonably expect a transition not toward eternal chaos, but toward a cellular structure.




## Chapter 9. WHY THE CELLS BECOME COLOSSAL

We have already arrived at the idea that in a dense vibrating medium, it is natural to expect the formation of stable regions—cells—in which pressure is distributed better than in a chaotic state.

But one more question remains.
If such cells arise, why should they be enormous?
Why not small?

**1. Small Cells Mean Many Boundaries**

Imagine a medium divided into very small regions.
What does this mean?
It means an enormous number of boundaries between them.
And every boundary is an imperfection.

At boundaries there arise:

— distortions of pressure,

— collisions of vibration regimes,

— losses due to rearrangement,

— local stresses.

In other words, boundaries are costly for the system.

**2. Nature Tends to Reduce the Number of Boundaries**

This is a general principle of many systems.
Soap bubbles merge into larger ones.
Droplets combine.
Crystal grains grow larger.

Small domains in materials redistribute.
Why?
Because fewer boundaries mean less stress.

**3. What Is More Advantageous for Aolian Space**

If the medium can “choose” between many small cells or a smaller number of large ones, then large ones are often more favorable.

Because then there is:

— less total boundary area,

— fewer zones of conflict,

— greater internal volume of the stable regime.

**4. Why the Sizes Can Grow Very Large**

It is important to understand that in aolian space there are no familiar limiting walls like those of a container.
The medium is infinite.

This means the process of enlargement can continue very far, until a regime is reached in which:

— the benefit of further growth decreases,

— vibrations are already stably distributed,

— neighboring cells are mutually balanced.

Therefore, the natural scale may become not “room-sized” or “planet-sized,” but cosmic.

**5. The Role of Vibrations**

If long-wavelength oscillations exist in the system, they themselves pull the structure toward a large scale.
A small cell is poorly matched to the slow and powerful oscillations of the whole medium.

A large cell can:

— contain major pressure regimes,

— smooth local noise,

— maintain stable internal dynamics.

**6. Why Not One Giant Cell**

One may ask: if larger is better, why not one single cell for the whole Universe?
Because an infinite medium cannot stably maintain one uniform regime everywhere.
Independent regions of coordination arise.
Thus a network of huge neighboring cells appears, rather than one universal cell.

**7. Why a “Small Universe” Is Still Colossal**

The name “Small” is relative.
It is small not by human standards, but in comparison with the infinite Universe.
Just as one cell is small compared with an organism, even though it may itself be a complex system.

**8. Main Conclusion**

The colossal sizes of cells in the aolian model are not an arbitrary fantasy.

They follow naturally from three reasons:

— it is advantageous for the system to reduce the number of boundaries,

— large regions are more resistant to internal vibrations,

— the infinite medium has no external scale limitation.

Therefore, from a chaotic dense medium, one should reasonably expect not a fine foam, but a network of vast stable cells.
